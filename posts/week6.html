Three ways to read a page

Every one of the 303 pages becomes a TF-IDF vector, using the course's definitions (tf = count / page length, idf = ln(303 / pages using the word)). A page's twin is the page with the highest cosine similarity to it. We did it three times, each time erasing one more thing that we suspected was doing the matching:

1 · Names keptThe raw page, every word in.
2 · Names erasedAny word capitalised in more than half its uses is a name and goes (9,026 words: Storm, Parker, Wakanda…). This is the course's week-6 filter.
3 · Names + pronouns erasedPass 2, and she, her, he, him, his go too. We added this after pass 2 surprised us (see below).

For every twin we also kept why they match: each word's share of the cosine, coloured name, pronoun or story. And we kept how far apart the pair is in the week-1 link network.

What makes two pages twins?

Each row looks at all 303 pages and their #1 twin. The bar shows what the match is made of on average. The two numbers on the right are what the twins have in common outside the text.

With names kept, twins are mostly just name-sharers. 89% of the average match is names, and 73% of twins are directly linked: of course, since a page that links to you mentions your name. Erase the names and something unexpected takes over. 85% of the 65 pages that mostly say she now get a twin who is also a woman, against 21% if twins were random. The words doing it are her and she. Most Marvel heroes are men, so a page full of her is a rare and very "distinctive" page, and TF-IDF rewards rare. Erase the pronouns too, and women's twins drop to 58%. That is still well above 21%. Some of the rest is real shared story: several of these women are X-Men telepaths, so mutants and telekinetic do the matching. Some is gendered vocabulary that isn't a pronoun, like female.

Long-lost twins (we read them)

Now the question we came for. In pass 3 (no names, no pronouns), which pairs read most alike while sitting 3 or more links apart, or not connected at all? Random pairs are that far apart 66% of the time, so being far is normal. Being far and textually close is the interesting part. We took the strongest far pairs and opened both pages every time:

Four of the seven are real. The text found something the links never recorded: two characters with replacement arms, two coming-out stories, two flyers on artificial wings, two vampire stories. One more is real but about the paperwork. The two fakes come from exactly the trap this week warns about. A name the filter missed (brain) and a word our tokenizer cut out of techno-organic are each enough to fake a twin. The cosine was no lower for the fakes: Bird-Brain & Brain Drain is the strongest far pair of all.

What surprised us

We erased the names to get at the story, and found gender instead. We expected that without names, the twins would be about powers and plots. Instead, the biggest single signal in pass 2 was the pronoun. In this corpus, being a woman is rare enough to be a page's most "distinctive" feature. That says less about TF-IDF than about who Marvel's superheroes are: 65 of 303 pages mostly use she.

What we checked. We read every pair in the hall in both pages' raw text (the quotes on the cards are pulled straight from the pages). We also read the Storm → Human Torch match, where 87% of the cosine is the word storm, and the pronoun claim: across all 303 pages, her and she carry 10% of the average pass-2 match, and much more for women's pages.

Limitation. Two of them, really. "Woman" here means "the page mostly says she". That's a shortcut that misses mixed pages (legacy names, teams), and nobody's gender is defined by a pronoun count on Wikipedia. And TF-IDF can't tell a theme from a coincidence: one rare word (techno, brain) can outweigh a whole page. A high cosine is a reason to open both pages, not a finding.
