The friendship paradox

Pick a random character: on average they're linked to about 10 others. Now follow one of their links to a random friend: that character averages about 22. Your friends are more connected than you — and it's not a trick. A character with 100 links is somebody's friend 100 times, so they turn up on the "friend" side of the sample 100 times as often as on the "person" side. The size of the gap is exactly the degree variance divided by the mean (σ² / ⟨k⟩) — which is huge for a hub-riddled network like this one.

It holds for 250 of the 286 non-isolated characters (87%). The 36 it doesn't hold for are almost all hubs — and the hubs are the whole reason everyone else loses the comparison.

Who drives it

One character is the single most-connected friend of everyone they know — all 106 of them. That's Spider-Man. He is never out-popularitied, and neither is Radian, the degree-6 hub of the 9-character island that never connects to the main universe — the island has its own little Spider-Man.

Compared to what? The shuffle test

"Marvel clusters 10× more than a random graph" sounds impressive, but a random graph has no hubs and the wrong degrees, so it can't tell you why. The honest baseline keeps every character's number of links fixed and scrambles only who links whom (100 degree-preserving shuffles). Here's what stays surprising and what turns out to be nothing:

Survivors — clustering (0.32 vs a shuffled 0.16, ~20σ), triangles, and reciprocity (39% of links go both ways vs 5% under the degree-matched null) — are real structure: the Marvel articles are written in groups that cite each other, and when A's page links B, B's page tends to link back. Casualties — the 17 isolates and the friendship paradox itself — are byproducts of the degree sequence: the shuffle reproduces them exactly, so there's nothing extra to explain.

Three fake Marvels

We grew a random graph, a small-world (Watts–Strogatz), and a rich-get-richer (Barabási–Albert) network, each with the giant component's 277 nodes and ~1,420 links. Every model gets about two of the three headline numbers — none gets all three.

Random nails the short paths and nothing else. Watts–Strogatz matches clustering and distance but its biggest "hub" has 14 links — Spider-Man has 106. Barabási–Albert is the only one that grows a real hub (61) and a heavy tail, but its clustering is a third of Marvel's. The missing ingredient in all three: communities — Marvel's triangles come from tight thematic clusters (the X-books, the Avengers, the street-level heroes) that no single-mechanism model produces.

What surprised us

The island has its own Spider-Man. Nine obscure characters — Radian, Scatterbrain, Toxyn and friends — form a component that never touches the main 277. Inside it, Radian (6 links) is the most connected, and none of his island-mates out-do him. The friendship paradox is scale-invariant: it re-runs in miniature inside a pocket universe nobody's heard of.
