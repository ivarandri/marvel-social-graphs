From links to relationships

For each of the 1,784 links A → B, we searched the story part of A's Wikipedia page (we stop at In other media and Reception, where the sentences are about films and critics) for sentences that mention B. That works for 1,464 links. The other 320 only link B from a part we cut, or call B by a different name (Psylocke for Betsy Braddock, say).

Then a small word list labels every sentence by the words near B's name:

Foe enemy · nemesis · rival · fought · battled · defeated · killed · attacked · captured · betrayed · kidnapped …
Ally ally · teammate · teamed up · partner · friend · mentor · alongside · helped · rescued · joined · trained …
Family married · wife · husband · son · daughter · father · mother · brother · sister · cousin · love interest …

A link gets the label with the most hits (ties are mixed; no hits is neutral). The result: 384 ally links, 194 foe links and 72 family links inside the main network, plus 666 neutral ones ("appears in the same issue as") that our words can't type.

Do enemies live next door?

Our guess was that foes would come from other corners of the universe: the X-Men fight cosmic villains, the street-level heroes fight mobsters. If so, foe links should cross community borders more often than ally links do. Here is the share of each type that crosses a border, against the same share when we shuffle the labels among the typed links 5,000 times:

Foes do cross a little more: 40% against 34% for allies and 32% for family. But shuffled labels give 36% ± 3%, so the foe gap is a z-score of only 1.35 (p ≈ 0.10). That's suggestive, not a finding. And it says something about the communities themselves: Louvain didn't find "heroes" and "villains". It found story worlds: Spider-Man's New York, the X-Men's school, the Hulk's gamma corner. Story worlds contain both your best friend and your worst enemy. Most Marvel feuds are local.

One caveat makes this cautious: about 1 in 4 labels is wrong (see below). Random mislabelling pulls the three bars towards each other, so the true gap could be somewhat bigger than the one we measure.

Everybody fights the Hulk

Count how often each character is named as somebody's foe, versus as somebody's ally (8+ typed mentions). Spider-Man is named most in both lists, because he's everywhere. But the share of foe tells a different story:

Two thirds of the Hulk's typed mentions are fights: 18 foe against 9 ally. We read all 18 and they're nearly all real. The Gargoyle is "the first enemy of the Hulk", Firebird "mistakenly battled the Hulk", Sabra's "first public act … is a battle with the Hulk", Rockslide is "one of the students who battle the Hulk when he attacks the institute", and Ghost Rider "decides to go to New York and fight the Hulk". Only two are wrong: Doctor Druid actually "teams with the Hulk against the Maha Yogi", and Doctor Strange is killed by Dormammu, not by the Hulk. At the other end, Nova (0 foe, 8 ally), Storm and Iron Fist are almost only ever named as teammates.

We read 120 of our own labels

A word list is a guess. So we drew 60 random labelled links, read each sentence, and wrote down what the relationship really was. The first version was only 60% right. Allies were fine (21 of 23), but foes were a coin toss (14 of 28) and family was a disaster (1 of 9). Three failures explained most of it:

The Black Widow problem"…the characters of Little Women (Black Widow, She-Hulk, Elektra)…" was labelled family because of the word widow. Four of the eight bad family labels came from names: Black Widow, Green Widow, "Black Widow's clone".
The team-up problem"…a team composed of Ghost Rider, Werewolf by Night, Gargoyle, Black Cat and Satana, to fight a mystically powered Jack O'Lantern." Satana is a teammate, but fight was 12 words away and counted.
Somebody else's family"Hawkeye believes he is retrieving his presumed-dead wife, the Mockingbird" sits on Patsy Walker's page. A word list labels the sentence, not the pair.

So we fixed what we could. Every other character's name is masked before matching (no more Black Widows). A foe word has to come just before the name ("battled the Hulk", "killed by Blade"). A family word has to be within three words. Links between two versions of the same character (Wolverine → Ultimate Wolverine) get their own label. Then we drew 60 fresh links that we hadn't tuned on:

73% right on the fresh sample. Foes improved to 2 in 3, and allies are about 4 in 5. Family is still only half right, and it's rare: just 73 links. Treat the family colour on the map as "a relative is mentioned nearby".

Frenemies (we checked)

Five pairs have a foe link one way and an ally link the other. We read all ten sentences, and only two pairs are real frenemies:

The other three are artefacts. In two of them the foe sentence is about somebody else's fight: Mania's page describes "the Venom symbiote's fight with Wolverine", and Star-Lord's page reports that Rocket is killed in a battle against a third party. In the third, the foe side is real (the Iron Man of 2020 was hired "to destroy Machine Man"), but the "ally" sentence is about Machine Man joining the A.I. Army "during the Iron Man 2020 storyline": a comic title, not the character. An honest frenemy count from our data is two, not five.

What surprised us

The communities aren't teams, they're neighbourhoods. We expected Louvain's groups from the link network to be "sides", with enemies across the border. Instead, foe links stay inside a community almost as often as ally links do. A Marvel community is a story world, and in a story world the hero and their nemesis share most of their pages. The second surprise was ourselves: our first word list called Black Widow a relative of half the universe, and we'd never have noticed without reading the sentences.

Limitation. We label a sentence, not a relationship. "X teamed up with B against Y" and "B's son was killed" both put relationship words next to B's name, even when the relationship is with somebody else. Our hand checks put the error rate at about one in four. Telling who did what to whom needs the grammar of the sentence (subject, verb, object), not just which words are nearby.
