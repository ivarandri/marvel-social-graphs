Week 7 · named entities, counted and checked

Whose page
is it anyway?

By Ívar Gunnlaugur Víðir  ·  The Wikipedia page of Bob, Agent of Hydra names Deadpool 22 times and Bob 18 times. So we asked of all 303 hero pages: who gets named the most on your own page, and which heroes live in someone else's shadow? Then we had to read 44 pages to find out how often our shadow detector was lying.

The shadow detector

1 · TagspaCy's small English model marks every name in every paragraph. We keep its raw output untouched, because it misses about half the character names (we measured that in exercise 7.8).
2 · ResolveMerge names we can justify (Peter Parker → Spider-Man), rescue codenames the tagger called places (Venom as a place, 537 times), and drop creators and comic titles.
3 · Count the heroThe page's own subject is counted by its names in the raw text: title, civil name, first and last name. This doesn't depend on the tagger.
4 · ShadowShadow = mentions of the most-named other character ÷ mentions of the subject. At 0.5 or more, you're in someone's shadow. At 1 or more, you're eclipsed.

Most pages are comfortably about their own hero: the median shadow is 0.15, so the runner-up gets about one mention for every seven of the subject. But 44 pages crossed the line on the first run. A computer would stop there. We opened every one of them.

44 suspects, 11 real shadows

Each bar segment is one reason a "stolen" page turned out fine, applied as one fix after another. Click a segment to see its pages, with the shadow before and after the fix.

Three out of four alarms were the instrument's fault, not the story's. The most common one was a hero "stealing" their own page under another name. Mania is "also known as Venom", Jericho Drumm is Brother Voodoo, and Black Cat is Felicia Hardy. Almost as common: pages about a codename that several characters share, where the "thief" is simply another holder. Nomad's page is mostly about Steve Rogers because Rogers was the first Nomad. Ricochet is an identity Spider-Man once wore.

Living in someone's shadow

The 11 pages that stay in a shadow after every fix. The gold bar is how often the page names its own hero, and the ink bar how often it names the shadow. Click a name to open the receipts below.

The biggest shadow casters are Deadpool (3 pages), Iron Man (2) and Captain America. And here's the twist for a network course: Iron Man and Captain America don't have pages in our 303-page network at all. The category we crawled in week 1 doesn't contain them. So the link network can't see the two characters these pages are written around. Only the text can.

What surprised us

We expected women's pages to be written through the men around them. They weren't. Only 1 of the 11 shadowed pages belongs to a woman (Anne Weying, in Venom's shadow): 9%, while women make up 21% of the pages. And the typical shadow on a woman's page (0.15) is the same as on a man's (0.14). The shadows we found are about sidekicks and spin-offs (Bob, Dogpool, Agent X around Deadpool; War Machine and Guardsman around Iron Man), not about gender. With 11 cases that's a weak "no", not a strong one.

What we checked. We read the lead of all 44 flagged pages and wrote down every fix, with the sentence that justifies it, in analysis/week7_post.py: extra names for 24 pages and holder lists for 22 shared codenames. The 11 real shadows were read in full. Their receipts are the sentences in the spotlight meter. That reading changed a result: our first run had War Machine eclipsed by Iron Man (1.24), until we noticed "James Rhodes" in the rival list. Rhodey is War Machine. Counted as himself, he's at 0.54: still in Iron Man's shadow, but no longer eclipsed. We also sampled 25 of the 801 mentions the title rule threw away. 23 really were titles (Iron Man #43, Venom: Let There Be Carnage). Two were list entries that do name the character ("Jean Grey: The most powerful…").

Limitation. The two sides of the ratio are counted differently. The subject is counted by string matching, which is generous. Everyone else comes from a tagger that finds only about half of all character names, and codenames least of all. That makes our shadows conservative: a rival known mainly by a codename the tagger can't see could hide a real shadow. And a mention count isn't importance. A sidekick's page should name the hero, so a shadow says how the page is written, not who matters more.
