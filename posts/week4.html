Meet the Sorting Hat

The network: every philosopher on Wikipedia's lists of philosophers born before 1900, with a link wherever one article links to another. Its giant component has 1,374 philosophers and 9,139 links. Our Sorting Hat is the Louvain algorithm. It sorts everyone into the groups that maximise modularity: groups with far more links inside them than you'd expect by chance.

It works. Louvain finds 8–10 houses with Q ≈ 0.50. We shuffled the network ten times, keeping everyone's number of links, and Louvain on the shuffles only reaches 0.22. The houses are real. But the hat is a randomised algorithm: it visits the philosophers in a random order, and a different order can give a different answer. So we put everyone under the hat 20 times. Each house is named after its most-linked member. Pick a philosopher and watch the hat make up its mind (or not):

Loading the hat…

Most philosophers are easy. Nietzsche is in House Kant 20 times out of 20, and Confucius heads his own house in 19 runs. The Buddha, Kant and Aristotle head a house in every single run. Plato is the surprise: in 8 of the 20 runs he leaves Aristotle and heads his own house. Teacher and student really are that hard to tell apart.

The hat's hardest cases

These are the philosophers (with 15+ links) whose 20 sortings disagree the most. Each bar is their 20 runs, coloured by house. Click one to put them under the hat:

The hardest cases aren't random. They're the Victorian science crowd and its fellow travellers: Darwin, Herbert Spencer, T. H. Huxley, Auguste Comte, George Eliot and Emerson. These are the people who brought evolution and positivism into philosophy. Their articles link evenly to the empiricists (House Locke), the Germans (House Kant), the scientists (House Descartes) and the 1900 generation (House Russell), so no single house can claim them. Darwin lands in six different houses in 20 runs. That's not the hat failing. It's the hat telling you that "which school was Darwin in?" is a bad question.

One number to keep us honest: two runs of the hat agree at a normalised mutual information of only 0.60–0.83. The borders move, even though the houses themselves are real. Using the link weights (how many times one article links another) gives a similar score (weighted Q 0.55), but agrees with the unweighted sorting at only NMI 0.64. That's why each card above also shows the weighted verdict.

Snip the threads of philosophy

Drawn in full, the network is a hairball. To see its skeleton we used the disparity filter. It keeps a link only if it carries a surprisingly large share of the links of at least one of its two philosophers. The knob is α. At α = 0.3 the backbone keeps 2,549 links (28%) and almost everyone is still in one piece. Lower α and only the most important ties survive.

Drag the slider down (or press snip) and watch philosophy come apart. We tracked the exact link that holds each tradition on to antiquity: when it goes, the whole tradition drifts off.

Hover a dot to see who it is.

The threads read like a history lecture:

  • Confucius–Voltaire is the last thread tying Chinese philosophy (22 philosophers still hanging together at that point) to the West. It goes at α = 0.17. Voltaire was the Enlightenment's great Confucius fan, and he held up Confucian ethics as morality without revealed religion.
  • B. R. Ambedkar–the Buddha is the Buddha's last thread (α = 0.16). Ambedkar, the Columbia-trained architect of India's constitution, converted to Buddhism in 1956 together with hundreds of thousands of followers.
  • Russell–Hegel ties Russell's circle to everyone else (α = 0.058). Russell started out a Hegelian and made his name rebelling against it.
  • Hume–Rousseau holds on the 117-philosopher piece with Kant, Hegel and Marx (α = 0.052). It's the Enlightenment's most famous quarrel: Hume sheltered Rousseau in England in 1766, and they fell out in public within months.
  • Augustine–Malebranche is the last thread between the moderns and antiquity (α = 0.047). Malebranche spent his life fusing Augustine with Descartes. Below this α, the ancient and medieval piece (68 philosophers) and the modern piece (40) are two separate networks.

So when the backbone "breaks", it breaks along real history. The things holding philosophy together are a fan, a convert, a rebellion, a quarrel and a synthesis, not the famous hubs.

Which house is Aristotle really in?

The hat says House Aristotle, 20 times out of 20. Obviously: he's the most-linked philosopher, so every house he's in gets named after him. That's the problem with sorting. Every philosopher gets exactly one house, and Aristotle is cited by everyone.

So we tried the other way round and sorted his links instead of him. Link communities group links whose endpoints have similar neighbourhoods, and a philosopher then belongs to every group their links end up in. Aristotle's 300 links fall into 47 rooms (plus 59 loose single links). Click a room:

Each spoke is one of Aristotle's links, grouped by room.

The answer: Aristotle isn't in a tradition. Traditions are in Aristotle. Just over half his links (158) are in one big room: everyone who cites Plato and Aristotle together. Of that room's 200 other members, 141 usually sort into House Aristotle; 38 are Christian thinkers (House Aquinas) and 20 are Islamic philosophers (House Avicenna). The other rooms are small and specific: the Franciscans, the Paris masters around the Condemnation of 1277, the Salamanca school, the Russian religious philosophers, the Victorian British scholars. And Plato is in almost every one of them.

What surprised us

The hubs don't hold philosophy together. Aristotle, Kant and Plato have hundreds of links, but their links stay inside their own traditions. Philosophy's traditions hang together by single links at unglamorous places: Voltaire's fondness for Confucius, a convert in 1950s India, and a quarrel in 1766. Remove them and the Western canon, India and China come apart. The hubs sort the network into houses, but the bridges between houses are held up by go-betweens.

Data: the course's frozen philosophers snapshot (15 Sept 2026). Louvain runs are unweighted, seeds 0–19. The link communities follow Ahn, Bagrow & Lehmann (2010), cut at maximum partition density. The disparity filter follows Serrano, Boguñá & Vespignani (2009); a link's α is the smaller of its two p-values, and the slider keeps every link with p < α. We implemented all three ourselves (no packages) and checked them against the course's numbers.
